\section{Ordering solvers}
\label{sec:ordering}

The question: holding the build plan fixed, how much does the
\emph{order} of execution matter?

\paragraph{Protocol.} Fix an economy plan (MCMC-annealed layouts on 10
seeded graphs), then choose the \emph{order} in which to execute its build
steps, paying each structure's cost from accumulated production. Scores:
\emph{accumulated production} (the integral of the production curve over the
build-out; rewards early payoff) and \emph{ticks to complete} the plan.

\paragraph{Reading.} Accumulated production is nearly flat across orderings
(spread $\approx 0.05\%$, far below the seed-to-seed sd) --- on these plans
the metric is dominated by the plan itself, not its ordering.
Ticks-to-complete discriminates sharply: \code{cheapest\_first} finishes
36\% sooner than naive \code{sequential} (2160 vs.\ 3379 ticks), with
\code{production\_gradient} --- greedily building whichever remaining step
most increases production --- second on completion (2792) while edging the
accumulation metric.

Verdict: \code{cheapest\_first} to get the plan online fast,
\code{production\_gradient} to front-load the production curve. Given the
early-war-collapse pattern (\S\ref{sec:elo-podium}), speed-to-online
plausibly matters more than the integral --- but the current headline metric
cannot adjudicate; a discounted objective $\int e^{-\lambda t} P(t)\,dt$,
which directly encodes the design notes' argument for average-time
optimization, is the proposed fix (\S\ref{sec:open}).
