\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:elo-protocol}

Full-game strength is the ground truth that every other section proxies. All
registered models compete except \code{v2\_knapsack} and \code{v3}, which are
code-identical to \code{v4}. Two protocols were run with the same harness,
seeds, and game count (312 games each; four per unordered pair, sides
alternated, deterministic seeds; logs under \code{results/elo\_duel/} and
\code{results/elo/}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Duel} (headline): symmetric 1v1 on a fresh Poisson map;
    winner by elimination, or by final node count at 5000 ticks. The standard
    basis for a pairwise rating.
  \item \textbf{Free-for-all} (secondary): three players --- one seat runs
    model A (``solo''), two seats run \emph{independent, uncoordinated}
    instances of model B; A scores against the better-performing copy. A
    majority-hostile environment rather than a duel; kept because it carries
    an internal control (copy vs.\ copy) and, as it turns out, discriminates
    where the duel cannot.
\end{itemize}

Ratings are fit per protocol by maximum likelihood under a Bradley--Terry
model \citep{bradleyterry1952} via minorization--maximization
\citep{hunter2004} on the aggregate score matrix, then mapped onto the Elo
scale ($1500 + 400\log_{10}p$) \citep{elo1978}. Unlike sequential K-factor
updates, the fit is independent of game order and exactly reproducible from
the logs.

The two protocols agree on every structural conclusion
($\rho = \ffaduelspearman$): the same podium
$\{\code{v8}, \code{v9}, \code{v12}\}$, the same \code{v11} crater at an
identical 0.17 score rate, the same knapsack-era basement. Mid-table shuffles
mildly --- \code{v0\_expansion} climbs to 4th in duels (pacifism does even
better with no third party to punish it) while \code{v10} slips to 8th.
Headlines:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The duel cannot separate the podium.} \code{v8}, \code{v9},
    and \code{v12} finish in an exact three-way tie (0.90 apiece): each
    crushes everything below and splits the games among themselves. The
    free-for-all \emph{does} separate them (0.90/0.85/0.75) --- its
    majority-hostile seat acts as an amplifier. The 1v1 is the honest basis
    for the rating; the FFA is retained as the tiebreaker environment.
  \item \textbf{\code{v8} shares the top} --- and it is the \emph{only} model
    using the distance-decayed local softmax, a branch abandoned when v9
    pivoted to OT transport. Combining \code{v8}'s softmax with \code{v12}'s
    transport remains the most obvious unexplored bot.
  \item \textbf{\code{v11} is a regression under both protocols} (0.17 in
    each, adrift from parent \code{v10} and child \code{v12}) --- dissected
    in \S\ref{sec:elo-v11}.
  \item \textbf{Pacifism is underrated}: \code{v0\_expansion}, which has no
    war agent at all, is 4th in duels, above every pre-OT warrior; the
    knapsack-era aggressors lose both tournaments outright.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{How transitive is the game? (HodgeRank)}
\label{sec:elo-hodge}

A scalar rating assumes the tournament is a gradient: everyone's strength
lies on one axis. The pairwise data can measure that assumption. Take the
skew-symmetric edge flow $Y_{ij} = (\text{score rate of } i \text{ over }
j) - \tfrac12$ on the complete comparison graph and Hodge-decompose it
\citep{jiang2011}: a gradient part (explained by a potential $s_i$, i.e.\ by
\emph{some} ranking) plus a residual, which on a complete graph is pure curl
--- local rock-paper-scissors cycles that no ranking can represent.

The result: the gradient explains \textbf{\hodgetransitivityduel} of the
flow's energy in the duel tournament (\hodgetransitivity{} in the
free-for-all) --- \textbf{roughly a fifth of the game is cyclic under either
protocol}, so the intransitivity is a property of the game, not of the
seating arrangement. The Hodge potential and the Bradley--Terry fit agree
exactly on order in both (Spearman $\rho = \hodgespearmanduel$;
Figure~\ref{fig:hodge}), so Elo is not mis-ranking anyone --- the cyclic
fifth is simply invisible to \emph{any} scalar rating. The pairwise score
matrix itself (in \code{results/}) is thus summarized by two objects: the
potential (Table~\ref{tab:elo}) and the largest curl cycles
(Table~\ref{tab:curl}).

\subsection{What drives the intransitivity?}
\label{sec:elo-cycles}

Two probes: a spectral one on the tournament we have, and a controlled
experiment on a hypothesis about \emph{why}.

\paragraph{The curl is one cycle, and it is organized by posture.}
The curl matrix is real antisymmetric, so it decomposes into independent
planar rotations --- each eigenplane is one cycle. Projecting the models onto
the leading plane (the ``gamescape'' construction of \citealp{balduzzi2019};
cf.\ \citealp{czarnecki2020}) shows a strikingly low-dimensional structure:
\textbf{the leading plane carries \cycleplaneshareduel{} of the curl energy}
--- essentially one game-wide cycle rather than many local ones
(Figure~\ref{fig:cyclic-plane}). And the phase ordering around that cycle
clusters by strategic posture: the war-first knapsack models sit together
(0--40$^\circ$), the OT podium together (88--116$^\circ$), and the
economy-first mid-table together (180--250$^\circ$). Intransitivity in this
game is not diffuse noise; it is a single rotation through posture space.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{\resultsfig{elo_duel/figures/cyclic_plane.pdf}}
\caption{Models on the leading cyclic plane of the duel tournament's curl
(distance from origin = participation in the dominant cycle; angular order =
position in it). Posture families cluster in phase.}
\label{fig:cyclic-plane}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Testing the rock--paper--scissors hypothesis.} A candidate
mechanism (Joel's, predating these experiments) maps the game onto RPS:
\emph{rock} = aggressive; \emph{paper} = defensive but distrustful (stacks
borders); \emph{scissors} = defensive and trusting (just builds). Predicted:
rock $>$ scissors (steamroll), scissors $>$ paper (leaner allocation), paper
$>$ rock (defender's advantage). The nearest existing models
(\code{v1\_knapsack}/\code{v0\_expansion}/\code{v12}) are useless for testing
this --- they differ in chassis quality (economy, transport era), and indeed
that triple is perfectly transitive in both tournaments (\code{v12} $>$
\code{v0} $>$ \code{v1k}, 8/8 each). So we built three archetypes on
\emph{one} chassis (v12's QUBO economy + OT transport,
\code{src/ai/models/archetypes.cpp}) differing only in posture, and played
the triangle (12 duels per pair, Table~\ref{tab:archetypes}).

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\resultstable{archetypes/tables/archetypes}
\caption{Archetype triangle, posture isolated on a fixed chassis. Score rate
is for the first-named archetype.}
\label{tab:archetypes}
\end{table}

Two of three edges confirm the theory --- rock sweeps scissors 12/12
(steamroll), paper sweeps rock 12/12 (defender's advantage) --- but the
linchpin edge fails: \textbf{paper also sweeps scissors 12/12, so there is no
cycle; border-stacking dominates}. The diagnosis is mechanical: in the
current rules, garrisoned troops have \emph{no opportunity cost} --- they are
not consumed, do not decay, and do not reduce production --- so ``resources
more efficiently allocated to building'' never cashes out. (One
implementation caveat: \code{war\_pool\_weight} scales the distribution pull
toward fronts but not the war agent's \emph{direct} attack commands, so
\code{arch\_paper} still attacks opportunistically; a clean test of
``defensive but distrustful'' wants an attack-suppression knob.) The RPS
wheel your intuition describes would light up in a variant where garrisons
cost something --- upkeep, or production diverted --- which is a game-design
lever, not a bot fix (\S\ref{sec:open}, item 7). Meanwhile the intransitivity
that actually exists lives higher in the stack: the podium's
softmax-vs-transport near-cycle of \S\ref{sec:elo-hodge}.

\subsection{Case study: the podium cycle}
\label{sec:elo-podium}

The podium's head-to-head structure cycles under both protocols; the
free-for-all makes it vivid. Figure~\ref{fig:v9v8} replays FFA tournament
game 1272 (\code{v8} solo vs.\ two independent \code{v9}). The game is
decided almost immediately: \code{v8} expands fastest off the start ---
peaking near 75 nodes by tick 250 --- but is torn apart in the first contact
wars and eliminated by tick $\sim$600. Everything after is the two \code{v9}
seats partitioning the map (the solid copy plateaus around 240 nodes and
wins the node count at timeout). The qualitative lesson: losses in this
protocol are not slow grinds but early-war collapses, so whatever edge
\code{v8}'s local softmax buys against the field, it does not survive
simultaneous pressure from two OT-supplied fronts on this map.

\subsection{Case study: the \code{v11} regression, with counterfactual}
\label{sec:elo-v11}

\code{v11} differs from \code{v10} by exactly one feature: OT demands are
netted against \emph{effective} (in-transit-inclusive) troop counts.
Figure~\ref{fig:v11} replays FFA tournament game 1301, which \code{v11}
lost, and then replays the \emph{same seed, same map, same slot, same
opponents} with \code{v12} substituted --- a single-variable comparison,
since \code{v12} is \code{v11} plus a standing frontline-garrison demand.

The contrast is stark. In the tournament game, \code{v11}'s trajectory is
nearly indistinguishable from the \emph{losing} \code{v10} copy --- both
expand to $\sim$100 nodes by tick 300 and are wiped out by tick 1000 while
the surviving \code{v10} sweeps the map. \code{v11} does not exhibit a
visibly pathological curve; it simply fights like a mid-tier bot and loses.
But substitute \code{v12} into the identical position and the same game is a
rout \emph{in the other direction}: all 347 nodes by tick 1000, both
\code{v10} copies dead by tick 900. One added demand term converts this map
from a loss into total domination, and in aggregate from a 0.17 score rate
to 0.75. The mechanism this implicates --- with netting alone, arriving
reinforcements cancel the very demands that summoned them, so contested
nodes stop pulling troops exactly when they need them, while \code{v12}'s
standing garrison demand keeps the pull alive --- fits the aggregate, the
counterfactual, and the protocol-independence of the 0.17, but remains a
hypothesis until the OT demands are instrumented directly in a replayed game
(\S\ref{sec:open}, item 2).
