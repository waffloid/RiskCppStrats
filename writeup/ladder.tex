\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:elo-protocol}

All registered models compete except \code{v2\_knapsack} and \code{v3}, which
are code-identical to \code{v4}. Two protocols were run with the same
harness, seeds, and game count (312 games each; four per unordered pair,
sides alternated, deterministic seeds; logs under \code{results/elo\_duel/}
and \code{results/elo/}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Duel} (headline): symmetric 1v1 on a fresh Poisson map;
    winner by elimination, or by final node count at 5000 ticks. The standard
    basis for a pairwise rating.
  \item \textbf{Free-for-all} (secondary): three players --- one seat runs
    model A (``solo''), two seats run \emph{independent, uncoordinated}
    instances of model B; A scores against the better-performing copy. A
    majority-hostile environment rather than a duel; kept because it carries
    an internal control (copy vs.\ copy) and, as \S\ref{sec:elo-ladder}
    shows, discriminates where the duel cannot.
\end{itemize}

Ratings are fit per protocol by maximum likelihood under a Bradley--Terry
model \citep{bradleyterry1952} via minorization--maximization
\citep{hunter2004} on the aggregate score matrix, then mapped onto the Elo
scale ($1500 + 400\log_{10}p$) \citep{elo1978}. Unlike sequential K-factor
updates, the fit is independent of game order and exactly reproducible from
the logs.

The two protocols agree on every structural conclusion
($\rho = \ffaduelspearman$): the same podium
$\{\code{v8}, \code{v9}, \code{v12}\}$, the same \code{v11} crater at an
identical 0.17 score rate, the same knapsack-era basement. Mid-table
shuffles mildly --- \code{v0\_expansion} climbs to 4th in duels (pacifism
does even better with no third party to punish it) while \code{v10} slips to
8th. Four headlines:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The duel cannot separate the podium.} \code{v8}, \code{v9},
    and \code{v12} finish in an exact three-way tie (0.90 apiece): each
    crushes everything below and splits the games among themselves. The
    free-for-all \emph{does} separate them (0.90/0.85/0.75) --- its
    majority-hostile seat acts as an amplifier. This tie is not merely a
    small-sample problem; \S\ref{sec:transitivity} shows the podium's
    head-to-head structure is genuinely cyclic.
  \item \textbf{\code{v8} shares the top} --- and it is the \emph{only} model
    using the distance-decayed local softmax, a branch abandoned when v9
    pivoted to OT transport. Combining \code{v8}'s softmax with \code{v12}'s
    transport remains the most obvious unexplored bot.
  \item \textbf{\code{v11} is a regression under both protocols} (0.17 in
    each, adrift from parent \code{v10} and child \code{v12}) --- dissected
    next, in \S\ref{sec:elo-v11}.
  \item \textbf{Pacifism is underrated}: \code{v0\_expansion}, which has no
    war agent at all, is 4th in duels, above every pre-OT warrior; the
    knapsack-era aggressors lose both tournaments outright.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Case study: the \code{v11} regression, with counterfactual}
\label{sec:elo-v11}

A ladder can flag an anomaly but not explain it; this is what the replay
tooling is for. \code{v11} differs from \code{v10} by exactly one feature:
OT demands are netted against \emph{effective} (in-transit-inclusive) troop
counts. Figure~\ref{fig:v11} replays FFA tournament game 1301, which
\code{v11} lost, and then replays the \emph{same seed, same map, same slot,
same opponents} with \code{v12} substituted --- a single-variable
comparison, since \code{v12} is \code{v11} plus a standing
frontline-garrison demand.

The contrast is stark. In the tournament game, \code{v11}'s trajectory is
nearly indistinguishable from the \emph{losing} \code{v10} copy --- both
expand to $\sim$100 nodes by tick 300 and are wiped out by tick 1000 while
the surviving \code{v10} sweeps the map. \code{v11} does not exhibit a
visibly pathological curve; it simply fights like a mid-tier bot and loses.
But substitute \code{v12} into the identical position and the same game is a
rout \emph{in the other direction}: all 347 nodes by tick 1000, both
\code{v10} copies dead by tick 900. One added demand term converts this map
from a loss into total domination, and in aggregate from a 0.17 score rate
to 0.75. The mechanism this implicates --- with netting alone, arriving
reinforcements cancel the very demands that summoned them, so contested
nodes stop pulling troops exactly when they need them, while \code{v12}'s
standing garrison demand keeps the pull alive --- fits the aggregate, the
counterfactual, and the protocol-independence of the 0.17, but remains a
hypothesis until the OT demands are instrumented directly in a replayed game
(\S\ref{sec:open}). Note for later: this is the first of three places the
frontline garrison will turn out to be decisive.
