\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:elo-protocol}

All thirteen models of Table~\ref{tab:lineage} compete. Two tournaments were
run with the same harness, seeds, and size (312 games each; four per pairing,
sides alternated, deterministic seeds):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Duel} (headline): symmetric 1v1 on a fresh random map;
    winner by elimination, or by node count at the 5000-tick limit. The
    standard basis for a rating.
  \item \textbf{Free-for-all} (secondary): three players --- the rated bot
    in one seat, two \emph{independent, uncoordinated} copies of its
    opponent in the others; the rated bot scores against the
    better-performing copy. A deliberately hostile environment; kept because
    it turns out to discriminate where the duel cannot.
\end{itemize}

Ratings are the Bradley--Terry fit of \S\ref{sec:bg-rating}, computed per
tournament from the logs under \code{results/elo\_duel/} and
\code{results/elo/}.

Both tournaments reach the same structural conclusions: the same podium
$\{\code{v8}, \code{v9}, \code{v12}\}$, the same \code{v11} collapse at an
identical 0.17 score rate, the same basement. Four headlines:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The duel cannot separate the podium.} \code{v8}, \code{v9},
    and \code{v12} finish in an exact three-way tie (0.90 apiece): each
    crushes everything below and splits the games among themselves. The
    free-for-all \emph{does} separate them (0.90/0.85/0.75) --- being
    outnumbered amplifies small differences. The tie is not just a
    small-sample problem: \S\ref{sec:transitivity} shows the podium's
    head-to-head structure is genuinely cyclic.
  \item \textbf{\code{v8} shares the top} using the one feature no later
    model kept --- the local softmax (Table~\ref{tab:lineage}). The most
    obvious unexplored bot is \code{v8}'s scoring plus \code{v12}'s
    transport.
  \item \textbf{\code{v11} collapsed, in both tournaments.} One feature
    separates it from its parent and one from its child; the case study
    below pins down which feature and why.
  \item \textbf{Never attacking is a decent strategy.}
    \code{v0\_expansion}, which has no war agent at all, ranks 4th in duels
    --- above every pre-OT model that fights. The eager attackers
    (\code{v1\_knapsack} and its hybrid) lose both tournaments outright.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Case study: the one-feature collapse of \code{v11}}
\label{sec:elo-v11}

The ladder's strangest datum: \code{v11} sits hundreds of Elo below both its
parent \code{v10} and its child \code{v12}. The lineage
(Table~\ref{tab:lineage}) makes this a controlled puzzle, because each step
is a single feature:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{v10} $\to$ \code{v11}: nodes stop requesting troops once help
    is on the way (troops en route count as delivered).
  \item \code{v11} $\to$ \code{v12}: border nodes additionally keep an
    always-on request --- the border reserve.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The experiment.} Games are deterministic given a seed, so we can
re-run a real tournament game and change nothing but the bot in one seat.
We take FFA game 1301 --- a map where \code{v11}, facing two \code{v10}s,
was destroyed --- and play it three times, once per lineage step, always in
the same seat against the same opponents. Figure~\ref{fig:v11} shows one
game per row; in every row, blue is the bot under test and green the two
\code{v10} opponents.

\paragraph{What happens.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\code{v10} in the seat (top row):} survives to the time
    limit holding about a quarter of the map. The seat is playable.
  \item \textbf{\code{v11} in the seat (middle row):} wiped out by tick
    1000. Its curve is indistinguishable from the \emph{losing} \code{v10}
    opponent's --- it doesn't malfunction visibly, it just loses.
  \item \textbf{\code{v12} in the seat (bottom row):} owns the entire map by
    tick 1000, both opponents dead.
\end{itemize}

Same map, same opponents, one feature per step: switching on
``stop requesting once help is on the way'' turns a playable position into a
loss, and adding the border reserve turns the same position into total
domination. The tournament aggregates match (score rates 0.65 / 0.17 /
0.75).

\paragraph{Why would such an innocuous feature be catastrophic?} Our working
hypothesis: a node under attack needs a \emph{continuous stream} of
reinforcements, but the moment the first ones are dispatched, its request
counts as filled and it goes silent --- so contested nodes stop calling for
help precisely while they are being ground down. The border reserve never
goes silent, which is why \code{v12} recovers. This fits the replay, the
aggregate, and the fact that the collapse is identical under both
tournament protocols --- but it is a hypothesis, not yet an observation.
The direct check (log each node's requests during a replayed loss) is
queued in \S\ref{sec:open}. Keep the border reserve in mind: this is the
first of three places it proves decisive
(\S\ref{sec:elo-rps}, \S\ref{sec:combat} are the others).
