\textbf{Benchmark.} A single player, production and combat disabled, must
redistribute troops from an initial to a target distribution on six preset
instances (star, path, bipartite, and three Poisson-map scenarios). Per tick we
record the $L^1$ loss to target; scores are \emph{ticks to converge} (first
tick with loss $<1$) and the \emph{loss integral} $\sum_t \mathrm{loss}(t)$ ---
lower means troops spent less time out of place, i.e.\ the average-arrival-time
criterion that motivates the Kantorovich objective
\citep{kantorovich1942,villani2009,peyre2019}.

Two solvers compete. \emph{Greedy} descends a potential field obtained by
solving a graph Poisson equation on the deficit --- the dynamical,
Benamou--Brenier-flavored view of the problem \citep{benamou2000}.
\emph{OT} solves a min-cost flow \citep{dantzig1963,orlin1997} with
successive-shortest-path/Johnson potentials \citep{johnson1977}, production
saturation via Frank--Wolfe \citep{frankwolfe1956}, the fixed-point scheme
first used for traffic assignment \citep{leblanc1975}. Load-dependent transit
(bigger groups move slower) places the true problem in the flows-over-time
regime of \citet{koehler2002}, which neither solver models exactly.

\textbf{Reading.} OT wins where structure matters: it converges nearly twice
as fast on the star ($136$ vs.\ $257$ ticks), is the only solver to converge on
the bipartite split, and carries a 7--9\% lower loss integral on the two
distributed Poisson scenarios. On \code{capital\_rush} (single sink) the two
are equivalent --- with one destination, gradient descent \emph{is} the
shortest path. The \code{path\_end} preset is anomalous: both solvers plateau
at a loss of 800 and never converge; this looks like a preset or solver bug
rather than a ranking signal and is flagged in \S\ref{sec:open}.
