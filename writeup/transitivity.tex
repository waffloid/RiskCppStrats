The podium tie of \S\ref{sec:elo-ladder} raises a sharper question than
``who is best'': how much of this game can \emph{any} single ranking capture?
This section answers it in four steps --- measure the intransitivity, find
where it lives, watch it in a single game, and test a hypothesis about its
mechanism. The chain ends somewhere unexpected: at a game-design lever rather
than a bot fix.

\subsection{Measuring it: HodgeRank}
\label{sec:elo-hodge}

A scalar rating assumes the tournament is a gradient: everyone's strength
lies on one axis. The pairwise data can measure that assumption. Take the
skew-symmetric edge flow $Y_{ij} = (\text{score rate of } i \text{ over }
j) - \tfrac12$ on the complete comparison graph and Hodge-decompose it
\citep{jiang2011}: a gradient part (explained by a potential $s_i$, i.e.\ by
\emph{some} ranking) plus a residual, which on a complete graph is pure curl
--- local rock-paper-scissors cycles that no ranking can represent.

The result: the gradient explains \textbf{\hodgetransitivityduel} of the
flow's energy in the duel tournament (\hodgetransitivity{} in the
free-for-all) --- \textbf{roughly a fifth of the game is cyclic under either
protocol}, so the intransitivity is a property of the game, not of the
seating arrangement. The Hodge potential and the Bradley--Terry fit agree
exactly on order in both (Spearman $\rho = \hodgespearmanduel$;
Figure~\ref{fig:hodge}), so Elo is not mis-ranking anyone --- the cyclic
fifth is simply invisible to \emph{any} scalar rating. The pairwise score
matrix itself (in \code{results/}) is thus fully summarized by two objects:
the potential (Table~\ref{tab:elo}) and the cycles, to which we now turn.

\subsection{Localizing it: one cycle, organized by posture}
\label{sec:elo-cycles}

The curl matrix is real antisymmetric, so it decomposes exactly into
independent planar rotations --- each eigenplane is one cycle. Projecting
the models onto the leading plane (the ``gamescape'' construction of
\citealp{balduzzi2019}; cf.\ \citealp{czarnecki2020}) shows a strikingly
low-dimensional structure: \textbf{the leading plane carries
\cycleplaneshareduel{} of the curl energy} --- essentially one game-wide
cycle rather than many local ones (Figure~\ref{fig:cyclic-plane}). And the
phase ordering around that cycle clusters by strategic posture: the
war-first knapsack models sit together (0--40$^\circ$), the OT podium
together (88--116$^\circ$), and the economy-first mid-table together
(180--250$^\circ$). Intransitivity in this game is not diffuse noise; it is
a single rotation through posture space.

\subsection{Watching it: the podium cycle in a single game}
\label{sec:elo-podium}

The podium's head-to-head structure cycles under both protocols; a replay
makes it concrete. Figure~\ref{fig:v9v8} replays FFA tournament game 1272
(\code{v8} in the test seat vs.\ two independent \code{v9}). The game is decided almost
immediately: \code{v8} expands fastest off the start --- peaking near 75
nodes by tick 250 --- but is torn apart in the first contact wars and
eliminated by tick $\sim$600. Everything after is the two \code{v9} seats
partitioning the map (the solid copy plateaus around 240 nodes and wins the
node count at timeout). Two lessons. First, losses in this game are not slow
grinds but \emph{early-war collapses} --- a pattern that recurs in every
case study in this report and explains why fast-payoff economies beat
gym-optimal ones (\S\ref{sec:economy}). Second, whatever edge \code{v8}'s
local softmax buys against the field, it does not survive simultaneous
pressure from two OT-supplied fronts: the cycle is real, and it turns on
\emph{which} strength a matchup happens to probe.

\subsection{Explaining it: testing the rock--paper--scissors hypothesis}
\label{sec:elo-rps}

A candidate mechanism (Joel's, predating these experiments) maps the game
onto RPS by strategic posture: \emph{rock} = aggressive; \emph{paper} =
defensive but distrustful (stacks borders); \emph{scissors} = defensive and
trusting (just builds). Predicted: rock $>$ scissors (steamroll), scissors
$>$ paper (leaner allocation), paper $>$ rock (defender's advantage). The
posture clustering of Figure~\ref{fig:cyclic-plane} makes this hypothesis
worth the experiment.

The nearest existing models
(\code{v1\_knapsack}/\code{v0\_expansion}/\code{v12}) cannot test it ---
they differ in chassis quality (economy, transport era), and indeed that
triple is perfectly transitive in both tournaments (\code{v12} $>$ \code{v0}
$>$ \code{v1k}, 8/8 each): power differences drown posture. So we built
three archetypes on \emph{one} chassis (v12's QUBO economy + OT transport,
\code{src/ai/models/archetypes.cpp}) differing only in posture, and played
the triangle (12 duels per pair, Table~\ref{tab:archetypes}).

Two of three edges confirm the theory --- rock sweeps scissors 12/12
(steamroll), paper sweeps rock 12/12 (defender's advantage) --- but the
linchpin edge fails: \textbf{paper also sweeps scissors 12/12, so there is
no cycle; border-stacking dominates}. The diagnosis is mechanical: in the
current rules, parked troops cost \emph{nothing} --- they
are not consumed, do not decay, and do not reduce production --- so
``resources more efficiently allocated to building'' never cashes out. (One caveat: our paper archetype
still launches attacks when it sees a guaranteed win --- the knob we turned
down governs where troops gather, not whether attacks fire --- so a purist
test awaits an attack-suppression switch.)

This closes the section's arc, and ties the report's threads together. The
posture wheel is two-thirds real, and its missing edge is a statement about
the \emph{game}, not the bots: standing armies are free. The same fact
appears twice more in this report --- the border reserve is what rescues
\code{v11} into \code{v12} (\S\ref{sec:elo-v11}), and what sweeps the
corridor map (\S\ref{sec:combat}). Under the current rules,
border-stacking is simply the dominant posture; give garrisons a cost
(upkeep, or production diverted) and the RPS wheel plausibly activates
(\S\ref{sec:open}). The intransitivity that exists \emph{today} lives
higher in the stack: the podium's softmax-vs-transport cycle, where the
strengths being probed differ by matchup rather than by posture.
