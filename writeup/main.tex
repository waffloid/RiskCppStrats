\begin{abstract}
CRisky is a real-time strategy game on graphs, and a family of bots that play
it by decomposing the game into solvable pieces. This report benchmarks all
of it. It asks four questions: which bot is strongest (a round-robin
tournament with proper ratings, read both as a ladder and as an ablation
study over the bot lineage); how much of the game any rating can capture
(about four-fifths --- the rest is rock-paper-scissors-like cycles, which we
locate and probe with controlled experiments); whether the component solvers
--- economy, transport, ordering, combat --- earn their keep in isolation;
and what the benchmarks reveal about the game itself (three mechanics with
design consequences). Statistics are paired with replayed single games
throughout, and every number and figure regenerates from committed code,
seeds, and manifests.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

This report exists to make CRisky empirical: wherever the codebase holds a
litany of competing proposals --- thirteen bot versions, four layout
solvers, two transport solvers, four build orderings --- it should be a
measurement, a score, and a ranking, not a hunch. The report is organized
around four questions, and each chapter answers one.

\emph{Which bot is strongest?} Chapter~\ref{ch:ladder} runs two round-robin
tournaments and fits a proper rating. Because the bot lineage grew one
feature at a time, the same tournament data supports a second reading:
each lineage step's head-to-head is an ablation, the marginal value of one
idea with everything else held fixed. The chapter ends on an anomaly ---
one mid-lineage bot sits hundreds of points below both its parent and its
child --- and dissects it with a controlled replay experiment.

\emph{How much can any rating capture?} A rating assumes ``better'' is
one-dimensional. Chapter~\ref{ch:limits} builds, slowly, the mathematics
for measuring that assumption --- rankings as altitude, cycles as the
obstruction --- and then applies it: about a fifth of this game is cyclic,
the cycles concentrate in a single wheel organized by strategic posture,
and a controlled archetype experiment traces the wheel to a game-design
fact.

\emph{Do the component solvers earn their keep?} Chapter~\ref{ch:components}
scores each engine component --- economy, transport, ordering, combat ---
in its own gym. Twice, the gym and the tournament disagree about what is
good; both disagreements teach something.

\emph{What did we learn about the game itself?} Benchmarking kept surfacing
facts about the rules rather than the bots. Chapter~\ref{ch:findings}
collects them, with next steps.

Two habits run through everything. First, every sweeping statistic is
paired with a zoom-in: single games, replayed by seed from the tournament
logs, so the anecdotes come from the same population as the statistics.
Second, gym scores are treated as diagnostics, never as objectives --- the
tournament is the only ground truth. Everything the report assumes --- how
the game works, how the bots decide, who the thirteen bots are --- is built
in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}; if a term is unclear anywhere, its
definition is there.

\section*{About the bots}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Build the missing bot.} \code{v8}'s local softmax and
    \code{v12}'s OT transport are the two strongest single features by the
    ablation ladder, they are compatible, and no bot has both
    (\S\ref{sec:elo-ablation}).
  \item \textbf{Confirm the \code{v11} diagnosis before reusing its
    feature.} The replay experiment of \S\ref{sec:elo-v11} pins the ladder
    collapse on ``stop requesting once help is on the way''; the proposed
    mechanism (nodes under attack go silent) should be confirmed by logging
    per-node requests in a replayed loss.
  \item \textbf{Scale the tournament.} Four games per pairing separates
    tiers, not neighbors --- the podium is an exact three-way tie. The
    harness is deterministic and parallel, and all analyses re-run on an
    extended log at no extra cost.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{About the game}
Three rule-level facts surfaced as side effects of benchmarking, each with
a design lever:
\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{3}
  \item \textbf{Parked troops are free.} Holding a large standing army costs
    nothing, so the border reserve dominates every other posture
    (\S\ref{sec:elo-rps}) and decides ladder ranks and battery maps alike.
    An upkeep cost would make the posture rock-paper-scissors --- currently
    two-thirds real --- live.
  \item \textbf{Large groups freeze.} Group speed falls with size, so a big
    blob can become effectively stationary: the \code{path\_end} staircase
    (\S\ref{sec:transport}) is exactly this, the flows-over-time effect of
    \citet{koehler2002}. A transport solver that splits mass into convoys
    would exploit the mechanic rather than suffer it.
  \item \textbf{Games are decided in the first thousand ticks.} Every case
    study is an early-war collapse; this is also why fast-payoff economies
    beat gym-optimal ones (\S\ref{sec:economy}).
\end{enumerate}

\section*{About the benchmarks}
\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{6}
  \item \textbf{Symmetrize the combat battery} --- the self-play control
    shows a decisive second-player advantage on every map
    (\S\ref{sec:combat}); play both seats per matchup.
  \item \textbf{Replace the ordering metric} --- accumulated production
    cannot separate orderings (\S\ref{sec:ordering}); discount it:
    $\int e^{-\lambda t}P(t)\,dt$.
  \item \textbf{Keep reading gym scores as diagnostics} --- the
    factory-bias paradox (\S\ref{sec:economy}) is the standing reminder.
\end{enumerate}
