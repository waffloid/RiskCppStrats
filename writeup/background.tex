% Background & methodology: everything the rest of the report assumes.

This section is the vocabulary for the rest of the report. It explains how
the game works, how the bots decide, what distinguishes each bot version,
and how we score things --- so that later sections can say what happened
without stopping to define terms.

\subsection{The game, in one paragraph}
\label{sec:bg-game}

Players compete for territory on a random graph. Every node can hold troops;
nodes with factories produce troops each tick, and powerplants boost
adjacent factories. Troops travel along edges (large groups move slowly),
and when opposing troops meet at a node they grind each other down until one
side is gone. A player loses when they have no troops left. There is no
diplomacy and no fog of war: the entire state is visible to everyone.

\subsection{How the bots decide}
\label{sec:bg-bots}

Every bot in this report has the same skeleton, with three stages each tick:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Score.} A few sub-agents each score every node for how much
    they would like troops there: the \emph{economy} agent scores nodes it
    plans to build on, the \emph{expansion} agent scores capturable border
    nodes, the \emph{war} agent scores enemy-adjacent nodes. The scores are
    blended into a single target: ``here is the fraction of my army I want
    at each node.''
  \item \textbf{Move.} A transport solver turns the gap between where troops
    are and where the target wants them into actual move orders. Older bots
    nudge troops downhill along a smoothed version of this gap
    (\emph{gradient transport}); newer bots solve a routing problem that
    assigns each surplus troop a destination and a cheapest path
    (\emph{OT transport}, for optimal transport).
  \item \textbf{Act directly.} Some decisions bypass the target-and-move
    machinery: the economy agent places a building the moment a node can
    afford it, and the war agent launches an attack the moment the
    neighborhood can overwhelm a target.
\end{enumerate}

Two terms recur constantly, so we fix them here:

\begin{description}
  \item[Request.] Under OT transport, each node asks for a specific number
    of troops (``I need 40 more''), and the solver routes troops to fill
    requests. The target distribution of stage 1 is expressed through these
    requests.
  \item[Border reserve.] An always-on request at nodes that touch enemy
    territory: keep enough troops here to outnumber the largest adjacent
    enemy force. Informally: park a standing army on the border. (The code
    calls this the \emph{frontline garrison}.)
\end{description}

\subsection{The ideas inside the components}
\label{sec:bg-ideas}

The lineage below names four ideas --- knapsack war, annealed layouts, local
softmax, OT transport --- each of which is a nice geometric or combinatorial
observation. This subsection traces them; everything later in the report
leans on these pictures.

\paragraph{Building is an anti-magnet problem.} A powerplant produces
nothing itself; it pays +2/tick to every adjacent factory. So powerplants
want to be surrounded by factories, and factories want at least one
powerplant neighbor --- like atoms that prefer unlike neighbors
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg-economy}, left). On an even cycle you can alternate the
two types perfectly; on an \emph{odd} cycle you cannot --- some edge always
joins two of a kind and is wasted (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-economy}, right).
This is exactly the physicists' \emph{frustrated Ising model}, and choosing
the best layout on a general graph is the MAX-CUT problem
\citep{goemans1995,lucas2014}: NP-hard, riddled with local optima that trap
greedy improvement. Hence the two solvers in the lineage: \emph{simulated
annealing} \citep{kirkpatrick1983} escapes local optima by sometimes
accepting bad swaps early on, and the Goemans--Williamson approach
\citep{goemans1995} relaxes each binary choice to a direction in space and
cuts with a random hyperplane. (The game's real objective is a weighted,
factory-biased version of pure alternation, but the geometry is the same.)

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/economy_ising.pdf}
\caption{The building problem. Left: the payoff motif --- one powerplant
(P) boosting every adjacent factory (F). Middle: on even cycles, perfect
alternation is possible. Right: on odd cycles some edge is always
``frustrated'' --- the combinatorial obstruction that makes layout
optimization hard.}
\label{fig:bg-economy}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Moving troops: a pressure field vs.\ a shipping manifest.} The
two transport generations are two classical pictures of the same problem
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg-transport}). \emph{Gradient transport} treats the
mismatch between where troops are and where they are wanted as a pressure
field: solve for a smooth ``height'' over the graph (a Poisson equation ---
the same mathematics as heat or electric flow \citep{benamou2000}) and let
troops roll downhill. Robust and cheap, but myopic: flow follows local
slope, so mass can dribble, split badly, or strand on plateaus.
\emph{OT transport} instead writes the shipping manifest explicitly: pair
every surplus troop with a destination so that total travel time is
minimized --- the Monge--Kantorovich optimal-transport problem
\citep{kantorovich1942,peyre2019}, solved exactly as a minimum-cost flow
\citep{dantzig1963,orlin1997}. The manifest knows global structure (send 60
\emph{there}, 40 \emph{further}); the pressure field only knows which way is
downhill. \S\ref{sec:transport} measures the difference.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/transport_views.pdf}
\caption{Two views of the same transport problem: one surplus (+100), two
deficits ($-60$, $-40$). Left: gradient transport --- a pressure field over
the graph, flow follows the slope. Right: OT transport --- explicit
consignments on cheapest routes.}
\label{fig:bg-transport}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Attacking is shopping with a budget.} Look along your border:
each adjacent enemy node has a \emph{price} (the troops parked on it,
marked up 20\% if fortified) and a \emph{value} (capital 5, artillery 3,
powerplant 2, factory 1, plus synergy bonuses). The troops on your frontier
are a \emph{budget}. Choosing which subset of targets to hit is then the
classic 0/1 knapsack problem (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-knapsack}) --- the
\code{v1\_knapsack} models solve it by value-per-price greed (and, for
benchmarking, exactly). Later war agents keep the same price/value scoring
but simplify the selection to a priority rule, preferring valuable targets
with few connections --- fewer ways for the enemy to reinforce them or
counterattack from them --- and attack any target the moment local supply
exceeds its price.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/knapsack_war.pdf}
\caption{The war agent's decision as a knapsack: frontier troops are the
budget; each reachable enemy node has a price (defenders) and a value
(structure worth); pick the affordable basket of maximum value.}
\label{fig:bg-knapsack}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Attention: one auction or many.} Stage 1 of every bot ends with
a softmax: node scores become shares of the army. A \emph{global} softmax
is a single auction --- the best-scoring target in the world attracts
troops from everywhere, however far away (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-softmax},
left). \code{v8}'s \emph{local} softmax discounts each target's pull by
distance, so nodes compete only with nearby rivals and each region feeds
its own best target (right). The effect is an army that fights on several
fronts at once instead of over-committing to the single shiniest
objective. This one change is, by the ladder of \S\ref{sec:elo}, the most
valuable feature any model tested --- and no later model kept it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/softmax_views.pdf}
\caption{Global vs.\ local softmax over the same scores (darker = higher).
Global: every node's troops flow toward the single best target. Local
(\code{v8}): pull decays with distance, so each region attends to its own
target.}
\label{fig:bg-softmax}
\end{figure}

\subsection{How we rate bots}
\label{sec:bg-rating}

Bots play round-robin tournaments (fixed seeds, both sides of every
matchup). To turn game results into a ranking we fit a Bradley--Terry model
\citep{bradleyterry1952}: assume each bot $i$ has a strength $p_i$ and that
$i$ beats $j$ with probability $p_i/(p_i+p_j)$, then find the strengths that
make the observed results most likely \citep{hunter2004}. We report
strengths on the familiar Elo scale \citep{elo1978}. This differs from
chess-style Elo updates only in being fit to the whole tournament at once:
the result does not depend on game order and is exactly reproducible from
the game log.

A one-number rating assumes strength is one-dimensional --- that if A
usually beats B and B usually beats C, then A usually beats C. That
assumption can fail (rock--paper--scissors), and \S\ref{sec:transitivity}
measures \emph{how much} it fails here: the tournament's pairwise results
are split into a part explainable by some ranking and a leftover made of
cycles (the Hodge decomposition of \citealp{jiang2011}), and the cycles are
then mapped out \citep{balduzzi2019}. The mathematics lives in
\S\ref{sec:transitivity}; nothing there is needed to read the ladder.

\subsection{Instruments}
\label{sec:bg-instruments}

Three kinds of evidence appear throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tournaments} rate whole bots (\S\ref{sec:elo},
    \S\ref{sec:transitivity}).
  \item \textbf{Gyms} score a single component --- economy, transport,
    ordering, combat --- in isolation, on synthetic instances
    (\S\ref{sec:components}). Gym scores are diagnostics: twice in this
    report a gym and the ladder disagree, and both disagreements are
    informative.
  \item \textbf{Replays} re-run a specific game from its seed, optionally
    with one bot swapped, to explain \emph{why} a number is what it is.
    Because games are deterministic given a seed, a replay with one bot
    changed is a controlled experiment.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Experiments live in
\code{experiments/<name>/run.py} (fixed seeds and solver lists; run as
\code{python3 -m experiments.<name>.run}). Each writes
\code{results/<name>/}: a \code{manifest.json} recording the git SHA, date,
command, and parameters; raw per-game CSVs; and the summary tables and
figures this document includes directly. To reproduce any number: check out
the SHA in the manifest, build, re-run the module. This round is a quick
pass --- game counts give stable rankings in minutes, not tight confidence
intervals.
