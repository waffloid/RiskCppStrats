% Background & methodology: everything the rest of the report assumes.

This section is the vocabulary for the rest of the report: how the game
works, how the bots decide, who the thirteen bots are --- one model at a
time, introducing each idea where the lineage first needed it --- and how we
score things.

\subsection{The game, in one paragraph}
\label{sec:bg-game}

Players compete for territory on a random geometric graph --- scattered
points joined to their near neighbors, so the map is mesh-like: triangles
everywhere, small dense pockets, no long corridors unless generated on
purpose. Every node can hold troops; nodes with factories produce troops
each tick, and powerplants boost adjacent factories. Troops travel along
edges (large groups move slowly), and when opposing troops meet at a node
they grind each other down until one side is gone. A player loses when they
have no troops left. There is no diplomacy and no fog of war: the entire
state is visible to everyone.

\subsection{How every bot decides}
\label{sec:bg-bots}

All thirteen bots share one skeleton, three stages per tick:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Score.} A few sub-agents each score every node for how much
    they would like troops there: the \emph{economy} agent scores nodes it
    plans to build on, the \emph{expansion} agent scores capturable border
    nodes, the \emph{war} agent scores enemy-adjacent nodes. The scores
    blend into a single target: ``here is the fraction of my army I want at
    each node.''
  \item \textbf{Move.} A transport solver turns the gap between where troops
    are and where the target wants them into move orders.
  \item \textbf{Act directly.} Some decisions bypass the target-and-move
    machinery: the economy agent places a building the moment a node can
    afford it, and the war agent launches an attack the moment the
    neighborhood can overwhelm a target.
\end{enumerate}

What distinguishes the models is which agents run, how scores become
targets, and which solver moves the troops. That is the story of the
lineage.

\subsection{The models, one at a time}
\label{sec:bg-lineage}

The lineage reads best as an \emph{ablation study}: each model adds
(approximately) one layer of capability to its predecessor, so consecutive
models measure the marginal value of one idea --- a structure
\S\ref{sec:elo-ablation} later turns directly into numbers. This walk
introduces each idea where it first appears; Table~\ref{tab:lineage} at the
end is the recap card.

\paragraph{\code{v0\_expansion}: the pacifist baseline.} Economy and
expansion agents only. The economy agent computes an ideal
factory/powerplant layout once, then works through it nearest-first; the
expansion agent pulls troops toward capturable neutral border nodes. It
never attacks another player. Everything after v0 is this bot plus war,
better layouts, better attention, or better transport.

\paragraph{\code{v1\_knapsack} (and \code{\_hybrid}): war arrives as a
shopping problem.} To attack well, look along your border
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg-knapsack}): each adjacent enemy node has a \emph{price}
(the troops parked on it, marked up 20\% if fortified) and a \emph{value}
(capital 5, artillery 3, powerplant 2, factory 1, plus synergy bonuses),
and the troops on your frontier are a \emph{budget}. Choosing which subset
of targets to hit is the classic 0/1 knapsack problem, which \code{v1}
solves by value-per-price greed (exactly, for benchmarking, when the
frontier is small). \code{v1\_knapsack} paid for its war agent by dropping
expansion entirely; the \code{\_hybrid} variant restores expansion at half
weight. Neither is competitive today, but the price/value/budget frame
survives in every later warrior.

\paragraph{\code{v4}: the workhorse.} Replaces the literal knapsack with a
priority rule: prefer valuable targets with few connections (fewer ways for
the enemy to reinforce them or counterattack from them), and attack any
target the moment local supply exceeds its price. This simplification is,
by the ablation numbers, the single largest jump in the lineage. \code{v4}
is the long-time default and the baseline everywhere in this report
(\code{v2}/\code{v3} are code-identical and excluded).

\paragraph{\code{v5\_qubo} and \code{v6}: the layout becomes a solved
problem.} A powerplant produces nothing itself; it pays +2/tick to every
adjacent factory. So powerplants want factory neighbors and vice versa ---
like atoms that prefer unlike neighbors (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-economy}a). On
the game's mesh-like maps this preference can never be fully satisfied:
a triangle joins three nodes in pairs, so two of them must match, and the
maps are full of triangles --- frustration is the generic condition, not an
edge case (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-economy}b). Formally this is the frustrated
Ising model / MAX-CUT \citep{lucas2014,goemans1995}: NP-hard, riddled with
local optima. \code{v5\_qubo} therefore picks its layout by
\emph{simulated annealing} \citep{kirkpatrick1983}, which escapes local
optima by sometimes accepting bad swaps early; the Goemans--Williamson
alternative \citep{goemans1995} relaxes each binary choice to a direction
in space and cuts with a random hyperplane. \code{v6} biases the objective
toward factories --- which \emph{lowers} its score in the layout gym yet
makes the bot stronger, a paradox resolved in \S\ref{sec:economy}.

\paragraph{\code{v7}: construction discipline.} One rule: stop expanding
while more than a fifth of your territory is unconstructed. No new theory
--- but note for later that this same feature helps on \code{v6} and
\emph{hurts} when re-added on the OT chassis (\S\ref{sec:elo-ablation}):
ablations are context-dependent.

\paragraph{\code{v8}: attention becomes local.} Stage 1 ends with a
softmax: node scores become shares of the army. A \emph{global} softmax is
one auction --- the best-scoring target on the map attracts troops from
everywhere, however far away (Figure~\ref{fig:bg-softmax}a). \code{v8}
discounts each target's pull by distance, so nodes compete only with nearby
rivals and each region feeds its own best target
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg-softmax}b): an army that fights on several fronts at
once instead of over-committing to the single shiniest objective. By the
ladder of \S\ref{sec:elo} this is the most valuable feature any model
tested --- and no later model kept it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/softmax_views/softmax_views.pdf}
\caption{Global vs.\ local softmax over the same node scores (yellow = the
two hotspots) on a game-like patch. Global: every node's troops pull toward

\label{fig:bg-softmax}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{\code{v9}: transport becomes optimal.} The two transport
generations are two classical pictures of the same problem
(Figure~\ref{fig:bg-transport}). \emph{Gradient transport} (v0--v8) treats
the mismatch between where troops are and where they are wanted as a
pressure field: solve a Poisson equation on the graph --- the same
mathematics as heat or electric flow \citep{benamou2000} --- and let troops
roll downhill. Robust and cheap, but myopic: flow follows local slope, so
mass can dribble, split badly, or strand on plateaus. \code{v9} instead
writes the shipping manifest explicitly: pair every surplus troop with a
destination so total travel time is minimized --- the Monge--Kantorovich
optimal-transport problem \citep{kantorovich1942,peyre2019}, solved exactly
as a minimum-cost flow \citep{dantzig1963,orlin1997}. Mechanically, OT
expresses the target through \textbf{requests}: each node asks for a
specific number of troops (``I need 40 more'') and the solver routes
supply to fill requests on cheapest paths.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/transport_views/transport_views.pdf}
\caption{Two views of the same instance on a game-like patch: one surplus
($+100$), two deficits ($-60$, $-40$). (a) gradient transport: nodes

\paragraph{\code{v10}--\code{v12}: refinements on the OT chassis.}
\code{v10} re-adds \code{v7}'s construction discipline. \code{v11} makes
requests subtract troops already \emph{en route} --- a node stops asking
once help is on the way. It sounds like bookkeeping hygiene; the ladder
says otherwise (\S\ref{sec:elo-v11}). \code{v12} adds the \textbf{border
reserve}: an always-on request at every node touching enemy territory,
sized to outnumber the largest adjacent enemy force --- informally, park a
standing army on the border. (The code calls this the \emph{frontline
garrison}.) The border reserve turns out to be decisive three separate
times in this report.

\subsection{How we rate bots}
\label{sec:bg-rating}

Bots play round-robin tournaments (fixed seeds, both sides of every
matchup). To turn game results into a ranking we fit a Bradley--Terry model
\citep{bradleyterry1952}: assume each bot $i$ has a strength $p_i$ and that
$i$ beats $j$ with probability $p_i/(p_i+p_j)$, then find the strengths that
make the observed results most likely \citep{hunter2004}. We report
strengths on the familiar Elo scale \citep{elo1978}. This differs from
chess-style Elo updates only in being fit to the whole tournament at once:
the result does not depend on game order and is exactly reproducible from
the game log.

A one-number rating assumes strength is one-dimensional --- that if A
usually beats B and B usually beats C, then A usually beats C. That
assumption can fail (rock--paper--scissors), and \S\ref{sec:transitivity}
measures \emph{how much} it fails here: the tournament's pairwise results
are split into a part explainable by some ranking and a leftover made of
cycles (the Hodge decomposition of \citealp{jiang2011}), and the cycles are
then mapped out \citep{balduzzi2019}. The mathematics lives in
\S\ref{sec:transitivity}; nothing there is needed to read the ladder.

\subsection{Instruments}
\label{sec:bg-instruments}

Three kinds of evidence appear throughout:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tournaments} rate whole bots (\S\ref{sec:elo},
    \S\ref{sec:transitivity}), and lineage-step head-to-heads within them
    serve as ablations (\S\ref{sec:elo-ablation}).
  \item \textbf{Gyms} score a single component --- economy, transport,
    ordering, combat --- in isolation, on synthetic instances
    (\S\ref{sec:components}). Gym scores are diagnostics: twice in this
    report a gym and the ladder disagree, and both disagreements are
    informative.
  \item \textbf{Replays} re-run a specific game from its seed, optionally
    with one bot swapped, to explain \emph{why} a number is what it is.
    Because games are deterministic given a seed, a replay with one bot
    changed is a controlled experiment.
  \end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Experiments live in
\code{experiments/<name>/run.py} (fixed seeds and solver lists; run as
\code{python3 -m experiments.<name>.run}). Each writes
\code{results/<name>/}: a \code{manifest.json} recording the git SHA, date,
command, and parameters; raw per-game CSVs; and the summary tables and
figures this document includes directly. Concept diagrams are likewise
script-generated (\code{writeup/figs/}), drawn on faithful miniatures of
the game's map generator. To reproduce any number: check out the SHA in the
manifest, build, re-run the module. This round is a quick pass --- game
counts give stable rankings in minutes, not tight confidence intervals.
